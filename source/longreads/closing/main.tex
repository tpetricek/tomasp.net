\begin{abstract}
Most software today is opaque and users have little insight into how it functions.
This means that users cannot adapt software to suit their needs, but it also leads to alienation
that hinders critical debates about software. The conception of a more open, democratic software
appears repeatedly throughout the history, ranging from the 1960s MIT hacker
culture and the Free Software movement to the visions behind Smalltalk and the early Web.
All of those movements had profound impact on the computing industry but, somehow, the conception
of open software that they envisioned always failed to materialize.

The aim of this article is to understand how software becomes opaque. I closely examine two
historical developments. First, I retrace a debate on information hiding and free software,
starting with the OS/360 project in the 1960s and concluding with the development of the Linux
operating system in the 1990s. Second, I follow the history of the Web from its academic and
vernacular origins in the 1990s throughout its professionalization in the 2000s.

The two studies suggest that software often becomes opaque because of well-intended engineering
and managerial decisions. Software developers aim to tackle the growing complexity of software,
find better ways of structuring it, or make software more efficient. Political and ideological
concerns become secondary in the reasoning and give way to engineering design decisions that
make software more opaque. To tackle the inflexibility and alienation of software, we need not
just new technical ideas but, more importantly, a framework for design decision making that does
not prioritize managerial and engineering decisions.
\end{abstract}
